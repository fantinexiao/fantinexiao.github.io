About Me

Hi! I am a third-year PhD candidate at the Erasmus School of Economics, Erasmus University Rotterdam, and Tinbergen Institute. My supervisors are Chen Li and Anne Boring.

I will be on the 2026-2027 academic job market.

My research focuses on uncertainty and emotional communication in social interactions. I study how beliefs and ambiguity attitudes shape people's decisions, and how communicating or perceiving emotions influences them.

To study these questions, I design experiments that incorporate emotional communication into economic games and measure beliefs and ambiguity attitudes alongside decisions.

I am also interested in topics related to gender, including gender stereotypes, fertility decisions, and childcare.

Work in Progress

Telling You How I Would Feel: Emotional Communication and Ambiguity in Trust

Job Market Paper

Abstract

Trust requires engaging with ambiguity about others’ reciprocity. Emotional communication may shape how people perceive and evaluate this ambiguity, yet many social environments constrain emotional expression. This paper examines whether communicating anticipated feelings affects willingness to trust. It considers three channels using the incentivized belief hedge method: belief, ambiguity aversion, and ambiguity-generated insensitivity. In a preregistered modified trust game, 350 U.S.-based Prolific participants are randomly assigned to one of four conditions: no message, happy message, disappointed message, or a choice between the two. Overall, emotional communication modestly increases expected returns but does not significantly affect trust or actual returns. Its effects on attitudes vary by emotion: happiness message increases ambiguity seeking, while disappointment message reduces a-insensitivity. These findings show that structured anticipated-emotion messages reshape how people perceive and evaluate ambiguity surrounding trust.

Anger as a Signal: Decomposing the Affective, Inferential, and Ambiguity Channels in Negotiation

with David Gonzalez-Jimenez and Chen Li

Abstract

Emotional expressions in negotiation, such as anger expressions, can influence strategic behavior, but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This paper develops a structural behavioral framework and uses an online experiment (N = 800) with a 2 × 2 between-subjects design varying the counterpart's anger expression and gender. We find that anger expressions have no significant overall effect on final offers because they reduce altruism while increasing beliefs about the counterpart's reservation price, with these two channels largely offsetting each other. Anger expressions also reduce perceived social ambiguity, especially when expressed by male counterparts, suggesting that male anger expressions are perceived as more diagnostic signals in negotiation.
